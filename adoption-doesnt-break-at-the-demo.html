Adoption doesn't break at the demo

August 2026 · 3 min read

The demo goes well. The engineers lean in. Someone says this could save us hours. Everyone nods. Then two months pass and nothing moves.

That gap is where adoption actually lives, and almost no one maps it. We obsess over the demo because it is visible and it feels like progress. But the demo was never where deals are won or lost. A good demo proves the tool can work. It says nothing about whether this specific team will run it, in their environment, on their real code, next quarter.

The people who love your demo and the people who block your rollout are usually not the same people.

The moment the room empties, the deal enters a funnel no vendor ever shows you. It has four stages, and each one kills more deals than any missing feature.

The first is the eval. A skeptical engineer takes the tool off the stage and points it at a real repository, the one with ten years of decisions baked in and no clean edges. The demo ran on a sandbox. The eval runs on the mess. If the tool cannot survive contact with the actual codebase, the story ends here, quietly, and you rarely find out why.

The second is the security and legal review. For any regulated team, the question is not does it work. It is where does our code go, who can see it, and can we prove it later. This stage ends the most promising deals, and it has nothing to do with how good the product is. A team that cannot answer the data question to its own compliance people will walk away from a tool it likes.

The third is the champion with no budget. Someone inside loves it. They ran the eval, they fought for it in the review. But there is no line item, no spending authority, and three other priorities ahead of yours. Enthusiasm without a budget owner dies on its own, slowly, and from the outside it looks like silence.

The fourth is the fit. The tool has to sit inside their stack, their CI, their permission model. Works in isolation is not works here. Every integration seam is a place the rollout can stall for reasons no one raised in the demo.

The pattern repeats. Vendors pour their energy into the one stage that is visible and already working, the demo, and ignore the four stages that are invisible, internal, and decisive. They polish the part the buyer already believed, and abandon the buyer exactly when the hard internal fight begins.

The fix has nothing to do with a better pitch. Go find the real blocker and help your champion clear it. Adoption inside a company is an internal campaign, and your champion runs it, not you. Your job is to arm them for a fight you never see: the eval plan that survives their worst repo, the security answers their compliance team will accept, the budget story their VP can defend. Give them that and they carry you through the four stages. Skip it and you lose deals you should have won, to the silence after a great demo.

Because the product was never really the blocker. The bridge was. The bridge from a tool that works to a team that runs it, through the eval, the review, the budget and the fit, is the actual work. Whoever builds that bridge wins the account. Whoever only polishes the demo keeps wondering why the room that loved them went quiet.
