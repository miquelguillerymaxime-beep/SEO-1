The tools became the work

August 2026 · 3 min read

It is 9 a.m. Before I talk to a single human, there is a queue. Feed the prompts. Fix the MCP that broke overnight. Reconcile two tables that disagree about the same account. None of it is selling. All of it has to happen before selling can start.

There is an old trap in business. The tools you build to streamline the work quietly become the work. The spreadsheet meant to track the process turns into a process of its own. The system meant to save time starts asking for time back. GTM has walked straight into it.

The tools you build to streamline the work quietly become the work.

Look at where a modern sales week actually goes. A rep is meant to sell, which means talking to people, understanding a problem, earning trust. Instead a growing share of the week goes to operating the stack that was supposed to make selling easier. Enriching records. Babysitting sequences. Wiring one tool to another that almost speaks the same language. Watching a dashboard that measures activity instead of progress.

Every piece arrived for a good reason. The enrichment tool closed a real gap. The sequencer fixed a real bottleneck. The AI layer promised to take the busywork away. On its own, each one made a defensible case. Nobody owned the sum. Add ten tools that each save an hour and you do not get ten hours back. You get a new full-time job, keeping the ten tools in formation. That cost never landed on a single invoice, so no one caught it.

The AI wave made this sharper. The promise was that agents would absorb the operational noise. In practice they added a layer. Now there are prompts to tune, context to feed, integrations to maintain, outputs to check before you trust them. The work did not vanish. It changed shape and moved upstream, closer to the rep, wearing the costume of leverage.

Here is my prediction. By 2027, reps stop tolerating it. Not because the tools are bad, but because the trade becomes impossible to ignore. The best sellers guard their time with customers most fiercely, and they will not keep spending it as unpaid systems administrators. They will demand that the operational noise disappears so they can get back to talking to humans. The teams that win the next cycle are the ones who hear that demand early and cut the stack back to what actually moves a deal.

Selling was never about the stack. It was always about the bridge to a person. A technical buyer decides to trust you in a conversation, not in a sequence. The stack was supposed to protect that time so more of it could go to the bridge. Somewhere it started eating the time instead, and the full dashboards let us call that progress.

The line worth drawing is simple. Some tools hand the conversation back to you. Others quietly take it away. Most teams have stopped telling the difference. Drawing that line again is the whole job for the next two years.
